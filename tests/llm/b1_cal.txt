Out on the far tip of the pier stands a lighthouse that has not needed a keeper since the nineties. The lamp turns by itself now, driven by a motor no larger than a shoebox, and a sensor decides when dusk has come. Still, an old woman climbs the iron stairs every Sunday. She wipes salt from the glass, checks the logbook nobody else reads, and writes one line: weather, wind, the number of gulls on the rail. When asked why, she shrugs and says the light deserves company.

A bicycle stays upright for reasons that took scientists a surprisingly long time to agree on. Riders steer into a fall without noticing, turning the front wheel toward the side they are tipping, which brings the wheels back under their weight. The geometry of the fork helps too: because the wheel touches the road behind the line of the steering axis, the frame tends to correct itself. Some test machines with no rider at all will roll down a slope and recover from a push, almost like a trained rider.

Our town holds its market on the first Saturday of each month. Farmers park their trucks in a square that is a car park the rest of the time, and by eight the stalls are crowded with crates of leeks, jars of honey, and cheese wrapped in waxed cloth. A fiddler plays near the fountain for coins. The fish seller shouts prices that change every hour, dropping them as noon approaches, so the patient shoppers wait and the hurried ones pay full price without complaint.

Snow changes the sound of a valley before it changes the look of it. The first flakes fall almost silently, yet within an hour the distant road has gone quiet, because fresh snow is full of air and swallows the noise of tyres and voices. Walkers notice they are speaking more softly without meaning to. By morning the drifts have rounded every fence post, and the only marks on the slope are the narrow tracks of a fox that crossed in the dark.

My grandfather kept every letter he received in shoeboxes under his bed. When we sorted them after he died, we found notes from a cousin in Brazil, postcards from a ferry captain he once worked beside, and a stack of envelopes addressed in pencil by a child who turned out to be my mother. Some letters asked for money, some offered it, and many only described the weather. Reading them in order felt like hearing half of a very long phone call.

Tomatoes are fussy plants. They want warm soil, steady water, and sunlight for most of the day, yet too much heat makes the flowers drop before any fruit can form. Gardeners pinch off the small shoots that grow where a leaf meets the main stem, hoping to push the plant's strength into fewer, larger fruits. A cracked skin usually means the watering was uneven, a dry week followed by a sudden flood, and the fruit grew faster than its skin could stretch.

The night train leaves the capital a little before eleven. Passengers share cabins with strangers, four bunks to a room, and the conductor collects tickets and hands out thin blankets that smell of soap. Through the window the suburbs thin into fields, then into dark hills where only a farmhouse light shows now and then. Around three the train halts at a junction for twenty minutes, and the silence wakes more people than the rattling ever did.

A mechanical clock is a controlled fall. A weight or a coiled spring wants to release its energy all at once, and the escapement lets it go one tooth at a time. Each swing of the pendulum unlocks the gear train for an instant and then catches it again, which is the ticking we hear. A pendulum's length sets the rate, so clockmakers adjust a small nut beneath the bob: raise it and the clock gains, lower it and the clock loses.

Bees find their way home by watching the sky's brightest point; they can still do it on cloudy days because they see the pattern of polarised light in the sky. A forager returning with nectar dances on the comb, walking a short straight line while shaking her body, then looping back to repeat it. The angle of that line tells her sisters which direction to fly, measured against the sun; how long the shaking run lasts tells them roughly how far.

The village library opens three afternoons a week and is run entirely by volunteers. Its shelves hold more cookbooks than novels, a quirk nobody can explain, and a whole wall of local history donated by a retired teacher. Children come after school to use the two computers and stay to read comics on the carpet. The librarian on Thursdays keeps a jar of boiled sweets on the desk and a list of who has borrowed what, written by hand in a ledger.

Bread rises because yeast eats sugar and breathes out gas. Kneading stretches the proteins in the flour into long elastic strands that trap the bubbles, so a well-kneaded dough swells like a balloon instead of leaking. Bakers let it rest in a warm place, punch it down, shape it, and let it rise again. In the oven the gas expands one last time before the crust sets, which is why the loaf grows taller during its first ten minutes of baking.

Fog rolled in from the harbour just as the ferry was due. The captain slowed to a crawl and sounded the horn every two minutes, a long low note that the passengers felt in their chests. On deck people stopped talking and peered into the grey, though there was nothing to see but their own breath. When the dock lights finally appeared, blurred and yellow, a cheer went up as though we had crossed an ocean rather than a bay.

Learning to swim as an adult is mostly a matter of trusting the water. Beginners hold their breath and tense every muscle, and their legs sink because a stiff body is heavier at one end. An instructor will ask them to float on their back with arms spread, ears under the surface, and simply breathe. After a few minutes most people discover the water will hold them up if they stop fighting it, and the strokes come more easily after that.

A potter centres the clay before anything else can happen. The lump is thrown onto the middle of the wheel, wetted, and pressed with both hands while the wheel spins, until it runs true and no longer wobbles against the palms. Only then can the potter open it with a thumb and draw up the walls. A pot made from clay that was never properly centred will twist as it rises, and no amount of skill later can rescue it.

Every autumn the cranes pass over our house on their way south. We hear them before we see them, a rattling call that carries for miles, and then the long wavering lines appear high overhead. The birds take turns at the front, where the air is hardest to push through, and drop back to rest in the slipstream of the others. Neighbours come out of their kitchens to watch, holding dish towels, and nobody goes back in until the last line has gone.

Maps lie a little, and they have to. The earth is round and paper is flat, so every map stretches something: areas, distances, or the shapes of coastlines. The familiar classroom map keeps angles true, which made it useful to sailors, but it swells the lands near the poles until a frozen island looks as large as a continent. Other projections keep areas honest and bend the shapes instead. Choosing a map means choosing which error you can live with.

The repair shop under the railway arch fixes almost anything with a plug. Its owner works behind a counter covered in screwdrivers, coils of solder, and radios with their backs taken off. Customers bring kettles that will not boil, lamps that flicker, and once a toy robot whose arm no longer waved. He charges by the job rather than the hour, and if a thing cannot be saved he says so plainly and returns it free of charge.

Ice on a lake does not freeze from the bottom up, and fish depend on that. Water is densest a few degrees above freezing, so as the surface cools in autumn the chilled water sinks and warmer water rises, until the whole lake reaches that temperature. After that, colder water stays on top, and ice forms there as a lid. Beneath it the deep water stays liquid all winter, dark and still, while skaters glide overhead without a thought for the trout below.

On the coldest evenings my neighbour lights a fire in a metal drum on the pavement and leaves a bench beside it. People stop on their way home from the bus to warm their hands, and conversations start that would never begin anywhere else. A delivery driver talks about his daughter's exams; a student explains her thesis on beetles to a retired plumber who asks sharp questions. When the fire burns low someone always adds a log, though no one can say who brought the wood.

Rope makers once worked in buildings a quarter of a mile long, walking backwards as they twisted fibres into yarn. The trick of a strong rope is that each layer is twisted the opposite way from the one inside it, so the tensions balance and the rope does not unwind when it is loaded. Sailors learned to coil it in one direction only, and to inspect it for worn patches before trusting it with a sail, a boat, or a life.

A good soup starts with patience rather than a recipe. Onions are softened slowly over low heat until they turn sweet and pale gold, which can take half an hour if nobody rushes them. Then come carrots, celery, a few cloves of garlic, and whatever is left in the bottom drawer of the fridge. Water or stock goes in, the lid goes on, and the pot is left to murmur on a low flame. Salt is added near the end, a pinch at a time, tasting between each, because a soup that is too salty can only be rescued by making more of it.
